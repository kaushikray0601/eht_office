Getting Started

4 questions

eTrace is a specialist design application for Electrical Heat Tracing (EHT) systems. It covers the complete EHT design lifecycle — from heat loss calculation and tracer selection, through cold cable sizing and single-line diagram generation, to cable schedule production and bill of quantities — all within a single, audit-ready workflow.

The application is built around two tracer technologies: Self-Regulating (SR) cables and Mineral Insulated (MI) cables. eTrace selects the most appropriate technology for each instrument line automatically, flags lines where the primary choice is not viable, and sizes the electrical supply cables to meet voltage drop and earth fault loop requirements.

eTrace is built for EHT design engineers, project engineers, and discipline leads who size and document heat tracing systems for industrial plants — refineries, petrochemical facilities, LNG terminals, power stations, and similar projects.

The application assumes working familiarity with EHT design principles: heat loss calculation, tracer technology selection criteria, circuit breaker sizing, and IEC or IEEE design standards. It is a professional engineering tool, not an introductory learning environment. The integrated Engineering Hub and Calculation Manual provide technical depth for less familiar topics.

Before you create a project in eTrace, gather the following from the project design basis:

  • Electrical supply — nominal voltage, frequency, and the minimum expected supply voltage (voltage variation).
  • Pipe schedule — pipe sizes (NPS/DN), insulation types and thicknesses, estimated or measured tracer lengths.
  • Temperature data — process temperature, minimum ambient temperature, and maintain temperature for each line.
  • Design standard — IEEE 515 or EN/IEC 62395, as agreed with the client.
  • Project safety factors — heat loss safety factor, voltage variation factor, spiral factor (if not default), and allowable voltage drop limit.
  • Maximum circuit breaker size — the largest MCB permitted in the distribution panel.

You can start a project with partial data and refine it progressively; eTrace tracks which lines are confirmed and which have outstanding inputs.

eTrace is currently in private beta. Access is by invitation only and is restricted to engineers working on projects where eTrace has been adopted as the design tool. If you believe you should have access, contact your project lead or discipline manager.

Heat Tracing Basics

5 questions

Electrical heat tracing (EHT) — also called trace heating or heat tape — uses electrical heating cables bonded to pipes and equipment to replace the heat that the pipe loses to the environment. It is used for four main purposes:

  • Freeze protection — keeping the pipe contents above 0 °C (or the fluid's freeze point) in cold ambient conditions.
  • Process temperature maintenance — holding a process fluid at its required operating temperature to prevent viscosity increase, crystallisation, or condensation.
  • Condensation prevention — keeping instrument lines and sample connections clear.
  • Thermal storage — compensating for heat loss during planned or unplanned plant shutdowns.

EHT is not a heating source — it is a heat compensator. It replaces what the insulation cannot fully retain.

Self-Regulating (SR) cables contain a conductive polymer core between two bus wires. As pipe temperature rises, the polymer's electrical resistance increases — the cable automatically reduces its heat output, preventing overheating and reducing energy consumption. SR cables can overlap without hot spots, are easy to install, and suit most freeze-protection and moderate-temperature maintenance applications (typically up to 65–150 °C depending on the product grade).

Mineral Insulated (MI) cables are constant-wattage heaters: copper alloy conductors packed in magnesium oxide insulation inside a metal outer sheath. Because they have no inherent self-regulation, the output is fixed and the circuit design must account for the full power at all times. MI cables are used where temperatures exceed SR limits (up to ~650 °C), where the hazardous area classification demands a fixed-wattage certified device, or where long circuit lengths and stable power delivery are priorities.

The two technologies are complementary — most large projects use both, with SR on the majority of lines and MI on high-temperature or long-run applications.

eTrace attempts SR selection first, using your validated SR catalogue. MI fallback is triggered — and flagged clearly — when any of the following apply:

  • The line's design temperature exceeds the maximum exposure temperature of all available SR products.
  • No SR cable in the catalogue can deliver sufficient watts per metre at the maintain temperature to offset the calculated heat loss.
  • The project design basis specifies MI as a preference for specific line types or temperature classes.

In every MI fallback case, the line is tagged MI fallback and appears as an open item on the Project Dashboard. An engineer must review and confirm the MI selection before the line can be marked as issued.

The cold cable (also called a power lead, supply cable, or connection cable) is the conventional electrical cable that runs from the distribution panel or junction box to the connection point where the heating cable begins. Unlike the heating cable itself, the cold cable runs through an unheated section and carries current to and from the heater.

Sizing a cold cable correctly matters for two reasons:

  • Voltage drop — too small a cable means the heater receives significantly less than the rated voltage, reducing heat output below design requirements.
  • Earth fault protection — the cold cable's impedance forms part of the fault loop; an oversized cable reduces loop impedance and improves protection device response.

eTrace sizes cold cables automatically from the heater current and run length, checks both voltage drop and earth fault loop impedance, and flags any cables that exceed project limits.

Heat loss (W/m) is the rate at which a pipe sheds thermal energy to its surroundings. eTrace calculates it from:

  • Pipe geometry — nominal pipe size and the resulting outer diameter.
  • Insulation properties — type, installed thickness, and thermal conductivity.
  • Temperature differential — the difference between the process maintain temperature and the minimum design ambient temperature.
  • Wind speed correction — surface convection increases with wind; a project-level wind speed is applied.

The raw calculated heat loss is then multiplied by the project heat loss safety factor (typically 1.10–1.25) to give the design heat loss used for tracer selection. This safety factor accounts for insulation ageing, moisture ingress, and construction tolerances.

Design Workflow

5 questions

eTrace is designed to be worked through in the following sequence, though earlier steps can be revisited at any point:

  1. Project Setup — define the design basis: supply voltage, heat loss safety factor, cable standard, allowable voltage drop, maximum circuit breaker size, and other project-wide parameters.
  2. Import Input Lines — upload or manually enter the pipe schedule: pipe sizes, insulation, temperatures, and estimated tracer lengths.
  3. Heat Loss Calculation — calculate and confirm heat loss for each line; lines with missing data are flagged.
  4. Tracer Selection — eTrace auto-selects SR cables from the validated catalogue; MI fallback is applied where SR is not viable. Review and confirm all selections.
  5. SLD Generation — eTrace builds the Single-Line Diagram, grouping heating circuits by panel and junction box.
  6. Cold Cable Sizing — eTrace sizes supply cables, checks voltage drop and earth fault loop impedance.
  7. Cable Schedule & BOQ — review, annotate, and export the schedule and material take-off.
  8. Dashboard Review — verify all gate checks are green before issuing the design package for construction.

The Single-Line Diagram (SLD) is the electrical distribution diagram for the entire heat tracing system. eTrace generates it automatically by grouping heating cables into circuits, each fed from a junction box connected to a distribution panel.

The SLD serves two functions: it is the primary electrical document issued to the construction team, and it is the data source from which cold cable lengths, circuit currents, and circuit breaker ratings are calculated. All subsequent cold cable sizing and cable schedule outputs are derived from the SLD topology.

Yes. The SLD Workspace provides a full set of topology tools:

  • Split — divide an overloaded circuit into two separate circuits.
  • Combine — merge two lightly loaded circuits to reduce panel feeder count.
  • Attach downstream JB — add a junction box downstream of an existing JB to accommodate long cold cable runs.
  • Reset — revert individual branches or the full topology to the auto-generated layout.

Every topology change is recorded in the audit history, including the previous state, so you can always trace what was changed and when.

The Cable Schedule is a tabular engineering document listing every heating circuit in the project with its key electrical parameters: tracer type and length, circuit current, circuit breaker rating, cold cable size, cold cable length, and voltage drop.

It is generated dynamically from the SLD topology and cold cable sizing results — there is no static version that can become outdated independently. Each row carries a review status:

  • Generated — calculated but not yet reviewed.
  • Review required — flagged for engineer attention (e.g., VD warning or MI fallback).
  • Checked — reviewed and verified, not yet released.
  • Issued — approved for construction.

The cable schedule exports to Excel and is the primary procurement and construction handover document.

The Bill of Quantities (BOQ) is the material take-off for the project. It summarises the total quantities of heating cable (by type and rating), cold cable (by conductor size), junction boxes, termination kits, and accessories required to construct the system as designed.

The BOQ is intended for procurement and cost estimation. It is derived directly from the design data and updates automatically whenever the SLD, cold cable sizing, or tracer selection changes. Export to Excel is available for handover to procurement teams.

Calculations & Standards

6 questions

eTrace supports two primary EHT design standards, selected at project level:

  • IEEE 515 — Standard for the Testing, Design, Installation, and Maintenance of Electrical Resistance Trace Heating for Industrial Applications. Prevalent in North American projects and projects following US engineering practices.
  • EN/IEC 62395 — Electrical resistance trace heating systems for industrial and commercial applications. Used on European and international projects.

For MI cable heater selection, IEC 62086 provides the framework for series resistance heater classification and application. Cold cable sizing follows IEC 60502 for cable conductor sizing and temperature ratings.

The full methodology, including equations and standard references, is documented in the Engineering Hub.

The heat loss safety factor is a project-wide multiplier applied to the theoretically calculated heat loss before tracer selection. A value of 1.15 means the tracer must deliver 15% more heat output than the calculated minimum.

It accounts for real-world uncertainties including:

  • Insulation moisture absorption and ageing (insulation thermal conductivity degrades over time).
  • Construction tolerances — gaps, imperfect contact between tracer and pipe surface, and minor insulation defects.
  • Local ambient conditions that may briefly exceed the design minimum temperature.

Typical values range from 1.10 to 1.25. A higher safety factor increases confidence at the cost of a larger tracer and higher circuit current. Agree the value with the client as part of the project design basis.

The voltage variation factor represents the minimum supply voltage the system must be designed to operate at, expressed as a fraction of nominal. A factor of 0.90 means the design must work correctly even when the supply drops to 90% of its nominal value — for example, 216 V on a 240 V system.

In practice, industrial supply systems typically vary ±5–10% from nominal. Designing at the low-voltage extreme ensures the heating cable still delivers adequate power during the coldest conditions, when a voltage sag coincides with maximum heat demand.

A value of 0.00 in the assumption panel means no voltage variation has been applied — the design assumes exactly nominal voltage throughout.

Self-regulating cables on small-diameter pipes, valves, and flanges are often installed in a spiral to increase the heat output per pipe metre (since a spiral delivers more watt-metres of cable per linear metre of pipe). The spiral factor is the ratio of cable length to pipe length required.

For example, a spiral factor of 1.3 means 1.3 m of heating cable is installed for every 1 m of pipe. This increases the delivered power (good) but also increases the total cable length for procurement and the current draw used in cold cable sizing calculations.

The spiral factor is used only when a spiral installation is specified — for straight runs the factor is 1.0. A single project-wide default value is set in the project design basis; individual lines can override it.

Voltage drop (VD) is the reduction in supply voltage that occurs along the cold cable due to the cable's own resistance. Because the heating cable is a resistive load, even a modest VD reduces its heat output — power is proportional to voltage squared, so a 10% VD causes roughly a 19% reduction in output power.

eTrace calculates VD as a percentage of nominal supply voltage and checks it against the project's allowable limit (typically 3–10% depending on the standard and client preference). Circuits that exceed the limit are flagged with a VD warning and cannot be marked as issued until the issue is resolved.

If VD is excessive, options include: increasing the cold cable conductor cross-section, repositioning the distribution panel, splitting the circuit into shorter sub-circuits via the SLD topology tools, or — where technically justified — accepting a higher VD with a documented engineering concession.

When a line-to-earth fault occurs in a heating circuit, the overcurrent device (MCB or RCD) must clear the fault quickly enough to prevent electric shock or fire. It can only do so if the fault current is high enough to trip the device within its rated disconnection time.

The fault current depends on the total earth fault loop impedance — the series sum of the supply source impedance, the phase conductor (cold cable), the heating cable, and the protective earth return path. A long, thin cold cable increases this impedance and reduces fault current, potentially preventing the protective device from operating in time.

eTrace calculates the total loop impedance for each circuit and verifies it against the project's protective device type (MCB curve type and rating, or RCD trip threshold). Circuits that fail this check are flagged and must be resolved before the design can be issued.

Results & Outputs

4 questions

The Project Dashboard is a single-page health summary for the entire project. It shows:

  • Design pipeline — a visual stage indicator from heat loss through tracer selection, SLD, cold cable sizing, and cable schedule, with the current completion status at each stage.
  • Tracer distribution — a breakdown of lines by tracer type (SR, MI, unselected) and status.
  • Cold cable metrics — voltage drop pass rates, earth fault loop pass rates, total circuit count, and any startup VD warnings.
  • Cable schedule status — rows issued, checked, in review, and generated.
  • Risk flags — active issues that require attention before issuing.
  • Thermal risk scatter — a chart plotting every line by its design temperature and heat loss rate, colour-coded by tracer type, to reveal concentration of high-risk lines.
  • Assumption exposure — a panel showing all project-level design factors so their impact on the design is visible at a glance.
  • Issue gate checklist — a structured checklist of conditions that must all be green before the design package can be issued for construction.

A row marked Review Required means the calculation result is complete but an engineering flag has been raised — most commonly a voltage drop warning, a startup VD warning, an MI fallback condition, or an earth fault loop failure.

These rows cannot be progressed to Checked or Issued status without an engineer actively reviewing and resolving (or accepting with justification) the flagged condition. This is intentional: it prevents calculation warnings from being silently passed over during document issue.

A row that is simply newly calculated and not yet reviewed is marked Generated — this is a neutral status, not a warning.

Each module has its own export function accessible from the module toolbar:

  • Input lines — exports to Excel.
  • Results — exports the heat loss and tracer selection results to Excel.
  • Cable Schedule — exports the full schedule (including annotations and review status) to Excel.
  • BOQ — exports material quantities to Excel.
  • SLD — exports the single-line diagram to PDF.
  • Verification Report — exports the full calculation audit trail to PDF.

All exports include a project header, revision block, and generation timestamp. They are intended to be attached to the formal design package.

The Verification Report is a structured calculation record that documents every assumption, input value, catalogue selection, and computed result for the project. It is intended to support:

  • Internal design review — a checker can verify calculations against the report without re-running eTrace.
  • Client and third-party audit — the report provides a transparent, traceable record of how every design decision was reached.
  • Project archive — a point-in-time snapshot of the design state issued for construction.

The Verification Report does not replace a formal engineering peer review or client approval process — it supports those processes by providing the necessary evidence trail.

Troubleshooting

5 questions

An MI fallback tag means eTrace could not find a suitable SR cable for this line in the validated catalogue, and has automatically proposed an MI heater instead. The most common reasons are:

  • The line's design temperature exceeds the maximum exposure temperature of every available SR product.
  • The required watts per metre (design heat loss) is higher than any SR cable in the catalogue can provide at the maintain temperature.

This is not necessarily an error — MI is the correct technology for many applications. However, you must review the proposed MI selection, confirm the heater type and parameters are appropriate for the line, and mark the selection as confirmed. The line will remain a blocking open item on the Project Dashboard until this is done.

A VD warning means the calculated voltage drop on the cold cable exceeds the project's allowable limit. The most effective remedies, in order of preference:

  1. Increase the cold cable conductor size — a larger cross-section reduces resistance and therefore VD. eTrace will recalculate automatically when you adjust the minimum cable size in the cold cable settings.
  2. Reposition the distribution panel — shortening the cold cable run is the most direct fix. This is a physical/layout decision that may require coordinating with the electrical layout team.
  3. Split the circuit — if the circuit has multiple tracers in parallel, splitting via the SLD Workspace reduces the current per cold cable, which reduces VD.
  4. Documented engineering concession — if none of the above is practicable, the VD exceedance can be accepted with formal justification, provided the heater's minimum operating performance at the reduced voltage is verified against the heat loss requirement.

When the calculated MCB rating exceeds the project maximum (set in the design basis), it usually means the circuit is carrying too many tracers or an individual tracer has unusually high current. Options:

  • Split the circuit — use the SLD topology tools to divide the overloaded circuit into two smaller circuits, each with a lower aggregate current.
  • Review tracer selection — if a single high-current tracer is the cause, check whether a lower-wattage SR cable or a different MI heater configuration could reduce current while still meeting the heat loss requirement.
  • Revise the maximum MCB size — if the project design basis maximum is overly conservative, discuss raising it with the client or discipline lead. This is an engineering decision, not a tool setting to adjust without approval.

Heat loss calculation requires a complete and confirmed set of input data for each line. Lines will be skipped if any of the following are missing or unconfirmed:

  • Nominal pipe size (NPS/DN).
  • Insulation type and installed thickness.
  • Process maintain temperature.
  • Minimum ambient design temperature.
  • Line status — the line must be set to Confirmed in the input module, not Draft.

Go to the Input module, filter for lines without a heat loss result, and check each one for missing fields. Once all required data is confirmed, re-run the heat loss calculation to update the results.

Catalogue entries in eTrace go through a validation process before they are used in design: each row is checked against the official manufacturer datasheet to confirm that wattage ratings, temperature classifications, and conductor data are accurate.

An unvalidated flag means this check has not yet been completed for that product family. This may occur because the manufacturer data is not yet available, or because the row was recently added and is pending review.

Do not issue a design that relies on unvalidated catalogue entries. Contact your project lead or the eTrace administrator to arrange validation before use. The Project Dashboard will flag this condition as a blocking issue gate item.
